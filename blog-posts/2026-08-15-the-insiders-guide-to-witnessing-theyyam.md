Dinner is over, but the night is only just beginning.

As the night deepens, the first drums begin to gather in the distance.

We make our way towards the temple.

The narrow approach roads are lined with cars, motorcycles, auto-rickshaws and the occasional bus. Some families have travelled from nearby villages. Others have returned from cities hundreds of kilometres away. Friends who now live in Bengaluru, Mumbai or abroad are back in the village. Everyone is heading towards the same temple.

There is a sense of anticipation, but nobody needs to be told why they are here.

Sometimes a volunteer from the temple committee announces what will happen next. Other times, you simply ask the person standing beside you.

Someone always knows.

People greet each other warmly. Families reunite. Children meet cousins they haven't seen in years.

For many, Theyyam isn't just a ritual.

It is also the time when home feels complete again.

I grew up in North Kerala, but I did not always see it the way I do now.

Like many things around us when we are young, Theyyam was simply part of home. Familiar enough that you don't always stop to ask why it matters.

Leaving Kerala and eventually coming back changed that for me.

Coming home gave me a different perspective. I began noticing things I had grown up around but had never really looked at.

Then I started introducing guests to Theyyam.

Seeing it through their eyes changed the way I saw it too.

The fascination when the first drums begin.

The questions afterwards.

The moment when someone who arrived wanting to photograph something spectacular simply puts the camera down and watches.

That is when I began to understand something I had taken for granted.

Theyyam is spectacular.

But the spectacle is only the surface.

WeTheExplorers isn't about showing people North Kerala.

It is about sharing the North Kerala we rediscovered when we came home.

**This is not a guide to watching Theyyam. It's an invitation to understand why it matters.**

## Seeing Beyond the Performance

The most common misunderstanding visitors have about Theyyam is an easy one to make.

They think it is a performance.

Something people go to watch.

And it is a performance. There are elaborate costumes, powerful movements, music, ritual and moments of extraordinary intensity.

But look around the performance, and you begin to see something else.

Families who have come to pray.

People who have travelled home from far away.

Volunteers moving around the temple.

Committee members making sure everything works.

Musicians waiting for their next ritual.

Performers who have travelled from one shrine to another through the season.

People who have come simply because this is their shrine.

People who have come to give thanks.

People who have come looking for reassurance.

Theyyam exists because of all of them.

That is why we don't begin our stories with attractions.

We begin with the people who give those attractions meaning.

There is a moment when the atmosphere changes.

For me, it is when the drums reach their peak.

The rhythm becomes more intense. The movements of the Theyyam become exaggerated and powerful. During some rituals, the performer runs through fire or embers, or jumps into fire, and the drums rise with the intensity of the moment.

When that happens, I am usually transfixed.

I am not looking around at the crowd.

I am watching the Theyyam.

Listening to the drums.

Feeling myself move with the rhythm.

Even after seeing Theyyam many times, there are moments when I feel myself becoming part of the divinity around the ritual.

You don't necessarily need to understand any of this before you arrive.

A visitor can feel Theyyam without knowing its history.

The sound of the drums, the movements, the crowd and the devotion around you are enough.

And no two Theyyams need to feel exactly alike.

The movements, props, costumes and pre-rituals can vary depending on the Theyyam and the lore behind it. Some may involve fire or other elements that are particular to that tradition.

There is no standard Theyyam performance to compare everything against.

Understanding can come afterwards.

Sometimes, that is the better order.

First feel.

Then ask.

Then understand a little more.

## It Takes a Village

A Theyyam night doesn't begin when the drums start.

The preparation begins much earlier.

Dates have to be confirmed for the year, sometimes changing slightly according to the Malayalam calendar. Other events around the Theyyam may need to be planned. Posters are prepared and shared through WhatsApp, social media and websites, as well as in the traditional ways. Funds have to be collected.

The temple committee takes care of much of this.

During Theyyam, some of the senior members can usually be found in the committee office on the temple premises. Others move around the grounds, each responsible for different parts of the event. They are often easy to recognise by the badge or bow pinned to their clothes.

Some temples also have a small announcement stall where committee members keep people informed.

But there are many more people involved.

There are volunteers everywhere.

Anyone who is willing to help can become part of making the event happen.

I've done this myself at my local shrine.

The community kitchen is one of the places where you see this most clearly.

Depending on what is happening, the kitchen may be working through the day and night. Breakfast, lunch and dinner are prepared, along with black tea and hot water.

Women from the locality are often among those preparing the vegetables. Volunteers help with serving.

People sit together.

You simply take whatever seat is available. You may end up next to someone you have never met.

Nobody seems particularly concerned about that.

People wait patiently in the queue.

Because the meal is more than a meal.

It feels like a temple blessing.

A local businessman may sponsor a meal as a way of supporting his community. A family may sponsor one as part of a prayer or as gratitude for a blessing they believe they have received. Others contribute to the common pool.

No one has to be asked to help.

People simply come forward.

I've been one of those people.

The performers themselves often arrive quietly.

Not everyone recognises them when they walk in.

They may arrive with their families. Wives and children can be around during the preparations too. It is one way the next generation grows up around a tradition handed down through generations.

During the Theyyam season, performers may travel between different shrines, following a disciplined lifestyle throughout the period.

The musicians are part of this world too.

When they are not performing, you may find them resting on mats or bedsheets spread across the shrine premises, corridors or open spaces.

People generally give them that space.

The one place where you may see a crowd gathering is around the face painting and costume preparation.

And then there are the priests.

They have access to the sanctum of the shrine or temple. They are part of the connection between the Theyyam and the deity.

The Theyyam itself does not enter the sanctum.

None of these people are there simply to support a performance.

They are part of the tradition.

## What Is Theyyam?

The first question many visitors ask afterwards is simple:

**"What exactly did I just see?"**

There is no short answer that can really do it justice.

You can talk about the costumes, the rituals, the music and traditions passed down through generations.

But you can also ask someone from North Kerala what Theyyam means to them.

The answer may have less to do with what you saw that night.

It may be about childhood.

About staying awake late.

About going to the shrine with friends.

About being part of a village gathering.

My father belongs to that generation.

Theyyam has been part of his life for as long as he can remember. He talks about the deities and rituals, but also about the memories of going for Theyyam as a child and being part of the village gathering.

He can recognise Theyyams from photographs and videos. He notices subtle differences in costumes and pre-rituals that I might miss.

For him, the timing of a Theyyam or the distance he had to travel was never much of a concern.

He simply went.

That is perhaps one of the hardest things for a visitor to understand.

Theyyam can be extraordinary and ordinary at the same time.

It can hold divinity, spectacle, memory and community, while still being part of the rhythm of everyday life.

After the performance reaches its peak and comes to an end, another part of the experience begins.

The blessing.

The Theyyam is usually seated on a stool or standing while people wait to approach.

Some speak to the Theyyam about their problems.

Some come looking for reassurance.

Some come to thank the gods for answering their prayers.

Some simply receive the prasadam and move on.

The emotions can be difficult to miss.

People become tearful. Some cry. Others fold their hands quietly.

And then, often, they return to their families and friends and share the prasadam or blessing they have received.

This is the part visitors can easily miss if they are still thinking about the performance.

The performance may be what catches your attention.

The blessing shows you why it matters.

## Arrive as a Guest, Enter with Respect

Before we take our guests to witness a Theyyam, we usually have a short conversation.

It isn't a list of rules.

It is simply about understanding the kind of place you are about to enter.

Theyyam is a religious ceremony, and people respect it as such.

Dress modestly.

Respect the space.

Leave your shoes where everyone else leaves theirs.

If you're unsure where to stand, look around at the local families. See where they are standing or sitting, and follow their lead.

Not the photographers.

Photographers are often looking for the best angle. That's understandable. But the best place to photograph Theyyam is not necessarily the best place to experience it.

Be aware of the space where the Theyyam is performing.

Don't step into it.

Don't block someone else's view.

And remember that the people around you may have come with prayers, family traditions and beliefs that are deeply personal to them.

Photography is part of the experience for many visitors.

We don't ask people to leave their cameras behind.

We simply ask them to use them thoughtfully.

Avoid flash.

Don't obstruct others.

And every once in a while, put the camera down.

A photographer is often looking for the best frame.

Someone without a camera can sometimes be more alive to everything else that is happening.

The drums.

The people.

The expressions.

The atmosphere.

Some of the moments that stay with you longest are the ones that never become photographs.

Once the Theyyam begins, we usually step away.

Not because we have stopped guiding.

Because we haven't.

We are simply giving our guests space to have their own experience.

I stay far enough away for them to experience the night without depending on me, but close enough that I can see them and be available if they need me.

I don't want to stand beside them explaining every movement.

Some experiences deserve a little silence.

Afterwards, they usually come back to me—or I go to them.

That is when the questions begin.

*"What was that?"*

*"Who was that Theyyam?"*

*"Why did people react like that?"*

Sometimes I know the answer.

Sometimes I don't.

And I'm comfortable saying that.

I may ask someone around us.

I may look it up later.

Taking someone somewhere does not mean pretending to know everything about it.

Sometimes the most honest answer is:

**"I'm not sure. Let's find out."**

## And Then Life Continues

The next morning, the shrine feels different.

Empty.

Calm.

Still there if you want to pray.

The priest may still be around. Committee members are winding down what remains to be done. Some volunteers are still helping. Locals for whom this is their shrine continue to come and go.

You may still see decorations and arrangements from the Theyyam.

But the crowds are gone.

The drums are quiet.

And life continues.

My father may spend the next morning resting.

Later, he might go to another Theyyam.

For the village, there usually isn't a long conversation afterwards about what happened.

Unless something particularly spectacular—good or bad—took place, there isn't much to discuss.

Because Theyyam is part of life.

It happens.

People participate.

They go home.

And eventually, they begin preparing for the next one.

That is perhaps what makes it remarkable.

Theyyam isn't kept alive by spectacular moments.

It is kept alive by ordinary people doing extraordinary things together, year after year.

And that isn't only Theyyam.

That is North Kerala.

## A Little More Than a Night

If this is your first Theyyam, you will probably remember the obvious things.

The colours.

The costumes.

The headgear.

The drums.

The fire.

You should.

They are unforgettable.

But Theyyam is not just something you watch.

It is something a community lives.

So arrive curious.

Enter with respect.

Watch before you try to understand.

Put your camera down sometimes.

Follow the people who belong there.

Ask questions afterwards.

And remember that you are entering a space that already has meaning long before you arrive.

**Respect deepens experience.**

When the night is over, you won't leave knowing everything.

You shouldn't.

One night isn't enough for that.

But perhaps you'll leave with better questions.

## The Guest

When the night is over, you may not remember every ritual you witnessed.

You may not remember the name of every Theyyam.

You may even forget some of the details of the costumes, the music and the fire.

But perhaps you will remember the people.

The families who came home.

The stranger who showed you where to stand.

The volunteer who served you a meal.

The person who waited quietly for a blessing.

The faces around you, and the feeling that you had been allowed into something that already belonged to them.

And perhaps, years later, when you think back to that night, what stays with you won't be what you saw.

It will be how you felt.

**Welcomed.
Curious.
Connected.**

Because for one night, you weren't simply watching North Kerala.

**You were a guest in someone's life.**
